# 2026-09-24 — `split` GATHERS ITS PIECES AND MAKES THE ARRAY ONCE, AND THE COLLECTOR STOPS ASKING WHICH VM IT IS

`csv` was the only row still slower than CPython after 0.1.6: **1.5x** on `run.sh`, every other row
ahead of Python. Its loop is `text.split("\n")` once a turn and `line.split(",")`, two `number`s and a
`.length` per line — 600,000 splits and 1.8 million pieces over the run. Sampled on a PGO build of dev
`bc0b522` (`bench/profile.sl`, the program run fifty times longer so `sample` could attach):

- **`gc$collect` 28.1%**, `run_frames` 18.7%, then **`Buf.grow<Value>` 5.3%** (two call sites, 178
  samples), `_tlv_get_addr` 3.7%, `_xzm_free` 3.2%, `trace_array` 3.1%, `parse_long` 2.9%,
  `trace_str` 2.3%.
- **`Buf.grow` was `split`'s answer growing from empty.** A `Buf` starts at eight slots and doubles, so
  a line of three fields allocated, filled and charged eight `Value`s, and `text.split("\n")`'s twenty
  thousand pieces were reallocated and copied at every doubling on the way to 32,768.
- **`_tlv_get_addr` was partly the collector**: `mark_payload`, which every tracer calls once per
  object it marks, read `current()` — a thread-local lookup per live object per collection, and the
  twenty thousand lines a parse turn holds are marked at every one of its ~5 collections.

What changed:

1. **`split` gathers into `Vm.split_pieces` and allocates the array once, at its size**
   (`do_split` in `text.sysl`). The gathering buffer keeps its room between calls, so after the first
   call it grows for nothing; it is a ROOT (`roots` in `collector.sysl`), which replaces the `hold` per
   piece — a collection inside a piece's allocation keeps what is gathered — and it is cleared only
   once the answer's own cell is made. A line of three fields now takes three slots and is charged
   three. *Counting the separators first and sizing from that was built and measured first: `split`'s
   own time went UP (71 → 77 ms under `SLATE_PROFILE`), the count being the cut's search done twice.*
2. **A tracer finds the VM from the heap it was handed** (`heap_owner` in `collector.sysl`): every
   heap slate collects is the `heap` field of a `Vm`, so the VM is at `offsetof(Vm, heap)` before it
   and `mark_payload(h, n)` needs no thread-local read.
3. **`trace_array` walks the items as one view** instead of one `Buf.at` per element.

Not built, and why: **the rest of `gc$collect` is in `sh.sysl.gc`, not here** — see *What is left*.

Control: a detached PGO build of dev `bc0b522`; branch `csv` at its last commit before landing. Both
`bench/pgo.sh`, sysl 0.0.132.

## The witness

`sample` of the long `csv` on the control and on the branch with changes 1 and 2:
**`Buf.grow<Value>` 178 samples → none**, `_tlv_get_addr` 117 → 91. `SLATE_PROFILE` on the program: 144 collections before
and after — the schedule is untouched, which is the point: the collector does the same work less
expensively and `split` makes less of it. Instruction counts are identical (no `Op` touched).

Tests: `tests_split.sysl` — a split's array has exactly room for its pieces (3 for three fields, 1 for
the empty text, 20,000 for twenty thousand); the gathering buffer is empty between splits and keeps its
room, and the next split sees only its own pieces; a split charges its pieces' bytes and three
`Value`s and nothing more; a collection forced in the middle of a split (a 256 KiB heap pre-filled
with garbage) keeps all 1,500 gathered pieces whole; `heap_owner` answers the program's own VM and a
second VM's, and a recount in the second lands on its books and none on the program's; every item of
a live 5,000-element array survives two collections. `tests/lang/splitpieces.sl` asks both back ends
for empty pieces, separators at either end, a multi-byte separator, non-ASCII pieces, a 10,000-byte
piece beside short ones, splits one after another, and that a split's array grows like any other.

## What is left

- **`csv` is 1.4x CPython and the collector is still ~30% of it.** `gc.collect` walks the object list
  FOUR times per collection: an ephemeron pass and a weaken pass over every object, live and dead,
  before the sweep and `rebuild_free` — and the first two find nothing to do unless the program has a
  weak map or a weak reference. Keeping a count of live objects whose kind has an `ephemeron` or
  `weaken` hook (raised in `hand_out`, lowered in the sweep, zeroed in `dispose`) and skipping both
  passes at zero removes two of the four pointer-chasing walks from every collection of every program
  that makes no weak value. **That is a `sh.sysl.gc` change and a gc release**, and it was not made
  here.
- CPython frees each piece the moment its count drops; slate traces the twenty thousand live lines
  at every collection of a parse turn and sweeps ~100,000 dead cells a turn. Past the gc passes, what
  closes that is fewer cells per piece or a cheaper sweep, not the schedule — raising `Headroom` or
  the floor is a memory dial and was not touched.
