# 2026-09-23 — `sh.sysl.gc` 0.2.5: the classifier is free, `alloc_raw` reaches one constructor, and `arrays` was never timing itself

**Three things were asked of this item and all three answered NO, which is the reason the page is
worth reading.** gc 0.2.5 (tag `f5a5b63`) landed two changes the package measured at 6.7% and 6.4% on
its own allocate-and-drop benchmark; picked up here they are **worth nothing at all** — geometric mean
**+0.51%** and **−0.09%** over two alternating best-of-9 runs, which is zero with the sign undecided.
And profile 5's note that `bench/arrays.sl` "is timing itself inside the measured region" is **wrong**:
that program reads no clock, and the 11.8% belongs to macOS's own allocator.

**What DID come out of it is a named speed item nobody had**, hiding under that mis-attribution: a
builtin **property** read (`xs.length`, `s.length`, `m.size`) still mallocs and frees a one-element
`Buf` per call, which `native-args` took off every other builtin call in 0.0.60. `arrays.sl` makes
**5,000,010** of them.

## The two halves of gc 0.2.5, and what each is worth here

| | what it is | package's own number | measured here |
|---|---|---|---|
| the size-class classifier | `bin_of`'s shift-and-compare loop becomes `leading_zeros` | 6.7% on allocate-and-drop | **nothing** |
| `alloc_raw` | an entry point that does not clear the payload | 6.4% on allocate-and-drop | **not reachable**, bar one cold constructor |

**The two runs disagree about the SIGN on fifteen of the twenty-three programs**, which is what says
this is noise rather than a small win read twice: `arrays` is −1.33% then +2.29%, `loops` −1.78% then
+2.43%, `csv` −2.89% then +0.13%. The two allocation-dense programs gc 0.2.4 moved 18.8% and 15.5% —
`alloc` and `calls` — read **+1.25/−0.97%** and **+1.62/−1.24%** here, straddling zero.

**Why it is zero, against profile 5's ~1% prediction.** That prediction was read off `gc$alloc`'s
sampled self time and the `+1224`/`+1152` offsets inside it. gc 0.2.4 had already taken `gc$alloc`
from 6.23% to 2.06% weighted; what the classifier holds of *that* is a handful of instructions on a
path an interpreter reaches once per object, and an object costs far more elsewhere. **The lesson is
the one this page keeps relearning: a share of a function that is already small is not a share of the
mean**, and a package benchmark that does nothing but allocate prices its allocator's internals at a
weight no consumer gives them.

Box was at 74–80% idle on run 1 and 73–85% on run 2, with another session's `java` alive throughout —
below the 85% the method asks for. That is a reason to distrust a ±1% reading and not a reason to
distrust **zero**: the alternating minimum is what survives a busy neighbour, both runs agree on the
magnitude, and a 6.7% win would be legible through far more noise than this.

### `alloc_raw`: the contract rules out nine of slate's ten constructors

`gc.alloc_raw(h, size, kind)` is `alloc` without the payload clear, under two conditions the caller
takes on. The first is the obvious one — **every byte is written before the next allocation and before
the next collection**, the collector being a reader the caller does not schedule. The second is the
one that decides this item:

> **No reference-counted member is assigned into it first.** Assigning a `string`, a `Buf` or a `Map`
> in sysl releases the previous occupant, and the previous occupant is garbage.

**Every slate heap object but one owns sysl storage outside the heap, and that is the design rather
than an oversight.** A slate string is a GC object holding a sysl `string` and is that string's only
owner; the same bargain gives an array its `Buf` and an object its `Map`, and the finalizers are built
on it. So:

| constructor | first counted member written | may use `alloc_raw` |
|---|---|---|
| `new_str_counted` | `s: string` | no |
| `new_big` | `n: BigInt` (owns a counted view of its limbs) | no |
| `new_array` | `items: Buf[Value]` | no |
| `new_bytes` | `data: Buf[u8]` | no |
| `new_object` | `entries: Buf[Entry]`, `index: Buf[usize]` | no |
| `new_weak_map` | the same two | no |
| `new_closure` | `params: Buf[string]` | no |
| `new_promise` | `message: string`, `waiters: Buf[usize]` | no |
| `new_env` | `names`, `mutable`: two `Map`s | no |
| **`new_weak_ref`** | **none — one `Value`, and a `Value` holds no counted member at all** | **yes** |

`new_promise` fails the FIRST condition as well, and that is worth knowing on its own: it does not
write `thrown`, relying on the zero `alloc` leaves. The ten `spare_*` reserves in `reset_heap` stay on
`alloc` for a third reason — they are allocated and not initialized at all, the constructor that is
handed one writing through it later.

**So `new_weak_ref` is what landed**, with `alloc_or_collect_raw` beside `alloc_or_collect` in
`obj.sysl` carrying the reasoning where a reader will meet it. It is provably sound — `target` is the
whole payload, written on the next line, and the only reader is `weaken_weak_ref` at a collection —
and it is worth **zero** on every benchmark, nothing in `bench/` making a weak reference.

**What was NOT done, and why it is a finding rather than a gap in this work.** The win is reachable:
slate's structs put their counted members FIRST, so an entry point that cleared only a leading prefix
— `alloc_zeroed_prefix(h, size, kind, n)` — would serve six of the nine, and zeroing 16 or 48 bytes of
a 40-to-96-byte payload instead of all of it is most of what `alloc_raw` offers. Doing it from the
consumer side instead means taking each counted field's address and clearing its bytes by hand before
the assignment, which is a contortion shipped as reading material in the one file where a mistake is a
bus error inside a tracer with no diagnostic. **That belongs in `sh.sysl.gc`**, and the measured
ceiling for it here is small: `memset` + `__bzero` was 1.87% weighted in profile 5, and the two
programs that would take it read zero above.

### The tests

Two, in `tests_weak.sysl` beside the other collector proofs, both over a **quarter-megabyte** heap so
that blocks are REUSED — over a large heap every allocation comes off the never-touched end, which is
zero anyway, and the test would pass against a constructor that had forgotten to write its field.

- `A_WEAK_REFERENCE_OVER_UNCLEARED_STORAGE_READS_BACK_WHAT_IT_WAS_GIVEN` — 240 rounds, each making a
  reference nothing roots, collecting, then building and reading the next one across a second
  collection: **480 collections**, every read of an object standing where a dead weak reference's
  bytes were, and every one answers what it was given.
- `A_FULL_HEAP_ANSWERS_A_WEAK_REFERENCE_WITH_ITS_RESERVE_AND_reset_heap_TAKES_ANOTHER` — the reserve
  path, which `alloc_raw` leaves exactly as it was, and `reset_heap` taking another after it.

## `arrays` AND `mach_absolute_time`: profile 5's note is wrong, and what is under it is real

Profile 5 wrote **"11.8% is the benchmark's own clock, not slate"** and put it on the struck list as
*"the program is timing itself inside the measured region"*. **`bench/arrays.sl` reads no clock.**
Neither does any other program in `bench/`, in any of the four languages — `grep` for `monotonic`,
`os.clock`, `Date.now`, `performance` and `time.time` across `bench/*.{sl,lua,js,py}` answers nothing.
Nor is it slate's own instrument: `profile_began()` is `if profile_on then monotonic().us else 0`, and
profile 5 was taken against a plain build with `SLATE_PROFILE` unset.

**`sample` names the caller, and it is macOS's allocator.** One 1 ms sample of `./slate
bench/arrays.sl`, 464 main-thread samples:

```
77 ???  (in slate)  load address … + 0x394af4
+ 51 _xzm_free  (in libsystem_malloc.dylib) + 1048
+ ! 50 mach_absolute_time  (in libsystem_kernel.dylib)
+ 14 _xzm_free  (in libsystem_malloc.dylib) + 352
+ !  7 __bzero        + 7 _platform_memset
+  9 _xzm_free       +  3 _free
25 sysl.buf$Buf.grow…  + 13 _xzm_xzone_malloc_tiny  + 6 _malloc_zone_malloc
```

`mach_absolute_time` is called **from `_xzm_free`** — macOS's xzone malloc reads the clock in its free
path — so the 11.8% is not a clock the benchmark asked for, it is **`free` itself**, and the whole
`free` node is 77 samples, **16.6% of the program**. `__bzero` and `_platform_memset` sit under it too,
which is libmalloc scrubbing rather than `gc.alloc` zeroing: **no `gc` symbol appears in that profile
at all**, which is a second, independent reason the `alloc_raw` half was never going to pay here.

**So the row was struck for the wrong reason and the right item was missed.** What allocates per turn
in that loop is `builtin_property` in `index.sysl`:

```sysl
builtin_property(target, name) match
    Some(p) ->
        var args: Buf[Value] = buf()

        args.push(target)

        return call_native_buf(p, args, at)
```

A `Buf` malloc, a push, a `call_native_buf`, and a free — **per property read**. `native-args`
(2026-09-20) took exactly that off every other builtin call and left this path on the buffer, listed
in `CLAUDE.md` among "the callers that genuinely have one". It does not have one: a property read has
exactly one argument, the receiver, and it is already standing on the operand stack.

**The size of it, from `SLATE_PROFILE=1`:**

| `arrays.sl` builtin | calls | µs |
|---|---|---|
| `length` | **5,000,010** | 203,216 |
| `push` | 500,000 | 22,321 |
| `print` | 1 | 21 |

Five million mallocs and five million frees to read a number a `StrObj`/`ArrayObj` already carries,
at 41 ns each against `push`'s 45 ns for real work. (Those microseconds carry the instrument's own two
clock reads per call and are a ratio rather than a wall figure; the sample above is the unencumbered
measurement.) **`length` and `size` are what a `while i < xs.length` loop calls once a turn**, which is
ordinary slate, so this reaches far past `arrays` — and there is no way to write round it, `len(x)`
having been removed from the language in favour of `.length`.

**It is left as an item rather than taken here**, this being one bounded piece of work and that being a
change to the field-read path with its own tests; the shortlist row now names it.
